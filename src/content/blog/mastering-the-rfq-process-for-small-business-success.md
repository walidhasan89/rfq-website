A strong RFQ process can help a small business win more deals without generating more leads.

Every Request for Quotation represents a customer who is actively considering a purchase. They already have a requirement, a deadline, and a reason to contact suppliers.

The opportunity is real.

However, many small and medium-sized businesses lose these opportunities because their RFQ workflow depends on crowded inboxes, spreadsheets, manual data entry, scattered attachments, and employee memory.

A request arrives through [Gmail](https://mail.google.com) or Outlook. Someone downloads the files, copies the product information, checks prices, builds a quotation, converts it into PDF, and replies.

That process may seem simple, but it becomes unreliable as RFQ volume grows.

Mastering the RFQ process means creating a repeatable system that helps your business respond faster, reduce quotation errors, protect margins, and deliver a more professional customer experience.

## What Is the RFQ Process?

**The RFQ process is the complete workflow used to receive, review, price, prepare, send, and follow up on a Request for Quotation.**

For suppliers, distributors, manufacturers, wholesalers, and B2B service providers, the process normally includes:

1. Receiving the RFQ
2. Reviewing customer requirements
3. Confirming specifications and quantities
4. Checking availability and costs
5. Calculating the selling price
6. Creating the quotation
7. Reviewing and approving the offer
8. Sending the response
9. Following up
10. Recording whether the opportunity was won or lost

The quality of each step affects the final result.

A competitive price may not win if the quotation arrives late. A fast response may still fail if it contains the wrong quantities. A professional PDF may not help if nobody follows up with the customer.

Success requires the complete workflow to work together.

## Why the RFQ Process Matters for Small Businesses

Large businesses may have dedicated procurement teams, sales operations employees, estimators, and specialized software.

Small businesses usually operate with fewer people.

The same employee may be responsible for:

* Reading customer emails
* Reviewing specifications
* Checking inventory
* Contacting suppliers
* Calculating prices
* Creating quotations
* Following up
* Managing existing customers

This makes efficiency especially important.

A slow or confusing RFQ process can consume a large portion of the team’s working day.

A strong process helps small businesses:

* Respond before competitors
* Reduce repetitive data entry
* Handle more quotation requests
* Improve pricing accuracy
* Avoid missing attachments
* Create consistent documents
* Follow up more reliably
* Understand why deals are won or lost

The goal is not only to process RFQs faster.

It is to turn more of them into profitable customers.

## Step 1: Capture the RFQ Immediately

The RFQ process begins the moment the request arrives.

Do not leave important opportunities unstructured inside a busy inbox.

Every new RFQ should be captured with basic information such as:

* Customer name
* Contact person
* RFQ reference
* Date received
* Submission deadline
* Products or services requested
* Estimated deal value
* Assigned employee
* Current status

This prevents the request from being forgotten.

### Assign clear ownership

Every RFQ should have one primary owner.

Several employees may contribute, but one person should remain responsible for moving the opportunity forward.

Without clear ownership, team members may assume that someone else is handling the request.

### Record the deadline immediately

Customers may include deadlines in:

* The email subject
* The message body
* A PDF attachment
* A spreadsheet
* A later reply

Confirm the exact deadline, including the time zone when relevant.

Set an internal deadline before the customer’s deadline. This creates time for final review and correction.

## Step 2: Review the Complete Requirement

Do not begin pricing after reading only the first paragraph of the email.

Review the complete RFQ, including every attachment and relevant message in the email thread.

Confirm:

* Product or service description
* Part numbers
* Quantities
* Units of measurement
* Technical specifications
* Required materials
* Quality standards
* Delivery address
* Required delivery date
* Currency
* Payment requirements
* Quote validity expectations
* Required documents

### Check attachment versions

Customers may send updated files after the original request.

Look for messages such as:

* Please use the new spreadsheet.
* The quantities have changed.
* Ignore the previous drawing.
* See the revised delivery schedule.
* The attached specification replaces the earlier version.

Using an outdated file can make the entire quotation inaccurate.

Clearly identify the latest approved version before pricing begins.

## Step 3: Identify Missing Information Early

An incomplete RFQ should not move directly into final pricing.

Missing details create assumptions, and assumptions create risk.

Common missing information includes:

* Exact quantity
* Material grade
* Product dimensions
* Technical drawing
* Delivery location
* Required date
* Currency
* Quality requirements
* Packaging instructions
* Approved alternatives

Contact the customer as soon as possible when information is missing.

### Ask specific questions

Avoid writing:

“Please provide more information.”

Instead, ask:

* Please confirm whether the required quantity is 500 or 5,000 units.
* Which drawing revision should we use for pricing?
* Should shipping be included in the quotation?
* Are equivalent materials acceptable?
* What delivery date is required?

Specific questions are easier for the customer to answer.

### Do not wait until the deadline

Discovering a missing specification during final review may force your team to rush the quote or miss the deadline.

Early clarification protects both speed and accuracy.

## Step 4: Qualify the Opportunity

Not every RFQ deserves the same level of effort.

Some opportunities are a strong match for your business. Others may have unrealistic deadlines, unsuitable specifications, or very little chance of success.

Before investing several hours, ask:

* Can we supply the requested product or service?
* Can we meet the required deadline?
* Is the expected order value worthwhile?
* Do we understand the customer’s requirements?
* Can we provide competitive pricing?
* Does the opportunity fit our capabilities?
* Is the customer likely to purchase?
* Are the commercial terms acceptable?

This does not mean ignoring small RFQs.

A small first order may lead to a valuable long-term relationship.

The purpose is to use employee time intentionally.

## Step 5: Build Accurate and Profitable Pricing

Pricing is one of the most important stages of the RFQ process.

The lowest price does not always win, and winning an unprofitable order is not success.

A complete price may need to include:

* Product or material cost
* Labor
* Supplier pricing
* Tooling
* Setup
* Packaging
* Shipping
* Duties
* Taxes
* Payment-processing costs
* Overhead
* Warranty risk
* Profit margin

### Confirm the correct quantity

Pricing may change significantly based on volume.

Check whether the customer is requesting:

* Prototype quantities
* An initial order
* Monthly demand
* Annual volume
* Several quantity options

When useful, provide quantity-break pricing.

For example:

| Quantity | Unit Price |
| -------: | ---------: |
|      100 |    [Price] |
|      500 |    [Price] |
|    1,000 |    [Price] |
|    5,000 |    [Price] |

This can make the offer more useful and encourage a larger order.

### Protect your margin

Employees should understand:

* Minimum acceptable margin
* Approved discount limits
* Shipping responsibility
* Currency risk
* Payment terms
* Quote expiration

A rushed quote may win the order but create financial problems later.

## Step 6: Create a Professional Quotation

The quotation is more than a price sheet.

It is a customer-facing sales document that represents your business.

A professional quotation should include:

* Company name and logo
* Contact information
* Customer details
* Unique quote number
* RFQ reference
* Issue date
* Expiration date
* Product or service descriptions
* Quantities
* Unit prices
* Discounts
* Taxes
* Shipping
* Total amount
* Payment terms
* Delivery terms
* Warranty
* Notes
* Assumptions
* Exclusions

### Make the document easy to scan

Customers may receive several quotations.

Use:

* Clear headings
* Organized tables
* Consistent spacing
* Short notes
* Visible totals
* Professional branding

Do not force the customer to search through dense paragraphs to understand the offer.

### Use a professional file name

Avoid:

* `Quote Final.pdf`
* `New Quote 2.pdf`
* `Final Updated Quote.pdf`

Use a clear format such as:

`CompanyName-Quotation-RFQ-1048.pdf`

This helps both your team and the customer manage the document.

## Step 7: Review Before Sending

Every quotation should receive a final review.

Check the document against the original RFQ.

Confirm:

* The customer name is correct.
* Every requested item is included.
* Product codes match.
* Quantities are accurate.
* The latest specifications were used.
* Unit prices are correct.
* Calculations are accurate.
* Currency is clear.
* Taxes and shipping are included or explained.
* Delivery terms are realistic.
* Payment terms are included.
* Quote validity is shown.
* The correct PDF is attached.

A short review can prevent expensive mistakes.

### Review high-risk quotations more carefully

Additional approval may be needed when the quote includes:

* Large discounts
* Low margins
* Custom products
* Long payment terms
* Expensive tooling
* Difficult delivery commitments
* Unusual warranties
* International shipping

Not every quote requires the same level of approval.

Create approval rules based on value and risk.

## Step 8: Send a Clear RFQ Response Email

Do not send only:

“Please see attached.”

Your email should help the customer understand what you are sending and what to do next.

A strong RFQ response email should include:

* The RFQ reference
* Confirmation that the quotation is attached
* A short summary
* Important delivery information
* Quote validity
* Any major assumptions
* A clear next step

For example:

“Thank you for sending RFQ #1048. Please find our quotation attached for the requested products. The quote includes unit pricing, estimated delivery, payment terms, and a 30-day validity period. Please let me know if you would like us to review alternative quantities or delivery options.”

Keep the email concise and helpful.

## Step 9: Follow Up Consistently

Sending the quotation is not the end of the RFQ process.

Customers may need time to:

* Compare suppliers
* Receive management approval
* Review technical details
* Confirm project funding
* Change quantities
* Negotiate terms

A professional follow-up keeps the opportunity moving.

### Create a simple follow-up schedule

Depending on the deal, the process may include:

* Confirmation shortly after sending
* First follow-up after two business days
* Second follow-up several days later
* Final follow-up before quote expiration

Do not send repetitive messages every day.

Each follow-up should offer value.

Ask whether the customer needs:

* Clarification
* Revised quantities
* Alternative products
* Updated delivery
* Different payment terms
* Additional documentation

### Track the next action

Every active quotation should have:

* Current status
* Last contact date
* Next follow-up date
* Customer feedback
* Required revision
* Responsible owner

Do not depend only on memory or inbox stars.

## Step 10: Record the Outcome

Every RFQ should eventually be marked as:

* Won
* Lost
* Cancelled
* Expired
* Delayed
* No decision

When possible, record why the deal was lost.

Common reasons include:

* Price
* Delivery time
* Product availability
* Payment terms
* Technical mismatch
* Slow response
* Existing supplier relationship
* Project cancellation
* No customer response

This information helps improve future quotations.

If most deals are lost because of slow response time, the solution may be operational rather than pricing-related.

## Common RFQ Process Mistakes

## Treating the Inbox as the Entire System

Gmail and [Outlook](https://outlook.live.com/) are communication tools.

They do not automatically create ownership, track stages, organize line items, or manage follow-up.

## Starting Pricing Before Reviewing Every Attachment

This increases the chance of using old quantities or incomplete specifications.

## Copying the Same Information Repeatedly

Entering customer and product details into spreadsheets, quotation templates, PDFs, and emails wastes time and creates errors.

## Building Every Quote From Scratch

Approved templates, saved company details, and reusable terms reduce document work.

## Focusing Only on Price

Customers also consider delivery, quality, availability, communication, and trust.

## Missing the Submission Deadline

A competitive quotation may never be reviewed if it arrives late.

## Forgetting Follow-Up

Many opportunities are lost because the conversation stops after the quote is sent.

## How to Measure RFQ Process Performance

Small businesses do not need complicated analytics.

A few basic metrics can reveal important problems.

### Quote Turnaround Time

Measure the time between receiving the RFQ and sending the quotation.

### On-Time Submission Rate

Track the percentage of quotations sent before the deadline.

### First-Time Accuracy

Measure how many quotes are accepted without correction.

### Quote Win Rate

Calculate the percentage of quotations that become orders.

### Average Quote Value

Monitor the typical financial value of each opportunity.

### Follow-Up Completion Rate

Track whether planned follow-ups happen on time.

### Lost Deal Reasons

Record why customers choose another supplier.

### RFQ Processing Time

Measure how many employee hours are required for each quotation.

These metrics show where the workflow needs improvement.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the main steps in the RFQ process?

The main steps are receiving the request, reviewing requirements, clarifying missing information, pricing, creating the quote, approving it, sending the response, following up, and recording the outcome.

### Why is the RFQ process important for small businesses?

A strong process helps small teams respond faster, reduce errors, protect margins, and handle more opportunities without increasing manual work at the same rate.

### How quickly should a business respond to an RFQ?

Simple requests may be answered within a few hours. Standard RFQs should often be completed within one business day, while complex requests should be acknowledged quickly with a realistic response timeline.

### What should be included in an RFQ quotation?

A quotation should include company and customer information, quote number, RFQ reference, products or services, quantities, prices, taxes, shipping, delivery, payment terms, validity, and relevant conditions.

### How can businesses avoid missing RFQ deadlines?

Record the deadline immediately, assign one owner, create an earlier internal deadline, and use a visible tracking process.

### Why do RFQ quotation errors happen?

They commonly result from repetitive data entry, outdated attachments, unclear specifications, wrong quantities, calculation mistakes, and rushed reviews.

### Should every RFQ receive a response?

Not necessarily. Businesses should qualify opportunities based on fit, value, feasibility, deadline, and commercial risk. However, every request should be acknowledged or closed professionally.

### How can businesses improve RFQ win rates?

Respond faster, use accurate pricing, create professional documents, address customer requirements clearly, and follow up consistently.

### Does RFQ automation replace sales or procurement employees?

No. Automation reduces repetitive administration. Employees still make technical, pricing, delivery, and commercial decisions.
