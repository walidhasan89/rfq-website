Comparing vendors should help your business make a better purchasing decision.

Instead, many small businesses end up with a crowded inbox full of PDF quotes, spreadsheets, unanswered questions, missing attachments, and prices that cannot be compared fairly.

One supplier includes shipping. Another excludes it. One offers a lower unit price but requires full payment in advance. Another provides better delivery terms but submits the quotation in a completely different format.

The problem often begins before the quotations arrive.

Without a clear RFQ template and a structured vendor evaluation process, suppliers respond based on different assumptions. Your team then spends hours reorganizing information before it can make a decision.

This guide gives you a free RFQ template and a simple six-step process for requesting, organizing, comparing, and evaluating vendor quotations.

## What Is an RFQ Template?

**An RFQ template is a reusable document or email format that businesses use to request pricing and commercial terms from suppliers.**

It standardizes the information sent to every vendor so the received quotations are easier to compare.

A professional RFQ template normally includes:

* Buyer information
* RFQ reference number
* Product or service requirements
* Quantities
* Technical specifications
* Delivery requirements
* Pricing fields
* Payment terms
* Quote validity
* Submission deadline
* Supporting documents

The purpose is not simply to ask for a price.

The goal is to receive complete, accurate, and comparable vendor quotations.

## What Is Vendor Evaluation?

**Vendor evaluation is the process of reviewing and comparing suppliers based on price, quality, delivery, experience, terms, reliability, and overall business fit.**

A proper evaluation looks beyond the lowest number on the quotation.

The cheapest supplier may have:

* Longer lead times
* Poor payment terms
* Limited warranty
* Expensive shipping
* Weak customer support
* Lower-quality materials
* Unclear delivery commitments

A slightly more expensive supplier may provide better long-term value and lower risk.

## Why Small Businesses Need a Structured Vendor Evaluation Process

Small businesses often manage supplier selection through [Gmail](https://mail.google.com), Outlook, spreadsheets, and separate PDF documents.

This may work for one or two quotations. It becomes difficult when several vendors submit offers in different formats.

A structured process helps your team:

* Collect the same information from every supplier
* Compare total costs fairly
* Identify missing details quickly
* Reduce purchasing errors
* Avoid hidden charges
* Evaluate delivery and quality
* Document the final decision
* Save time on future purchases

It also creates a more professional experience for suppliers.

Clear requirements help serious vendors prepare stronger quotations.

## Step 1: Define What You Actually Need

Before contacting suppliers, define the purchase as clearly as possible.

A vendor cannot provide an accurate quotation when the buyer has not confirmed the product, service, quantity, or delivery requirement.

Start by answering a few basic questions:

* What are we buying?
* How many units do we need?
* Which specifications are required?
* Are alternatives allowed?
* Where must the order be delivered?
* When is delivery required?
* Which quality standards apply?
* Is this a one-time or recurring purchase?

### Define the product or service

For products, include:

* Product name
* Part number
* Model
* Brand or approved equivalent
* Material
* Size
* Color
* Dimensions
* Technical requirements
* Required certifications

For services, include:

* Scope of work
* Required tasks
* Deliverables
* Service locations
* Expected schedule
* Required experience
* Performance requirements
* Support expectations

Avoid descriptions such as:

* High-quality product
* Standard size
* Similar to our current item
* Regular maintenance service
* Fast delivery required

These phrases are too open to interpretation.

### Confirm the quantity

Quantity directly affects price.

A supplier may quote differently for 100, 1,000, or 10,000 units because material costs, setup time, labor, packaging, and shipping change.

When the final volume is uncertain, ask for quantity breaks.

For example:

* 100 units
* 500 units
* 1,000 units
* 5,000 units

This helps your business understand volume discounts before making a commitment.

### Separate essential and preferred requirements

Not every requirement has the same importance.

Mark each one as:

* Mandatory
* Preferred
* Optional

This makes vendor evaluation easier.

A supplier that fails a mandatory requirement may be removed from consideration, while a missing optional feature may only affect its score.

## Step 2: Send Every Vendor the Same RFQ

Once your requirements are clear, send the same RFQ to every participating supplier.

This creates a fair comparison.

When vendors receive different instructions, their quotations may be based on different products, service levels, shipping terms, or assumptions.

### Use a unique RFQ reference

Assign every request a number.

Examples include:

* RFQ-2026-001
* RFQ-PROC-1048
* RFQ-WH-026

Use the same reference in:

* Email subject lines
* RFQ documents
* Attachment names
* Supplier responses
* Evaluation spreadsheets
* Purchase orders

This prevents documents from different requests becoming mixed together.

### Include clear submission instructions

Tell suppliers exactly how to respond.

Include:

* Submission deadline
* Email address
* Required subject line
* Accepted file format
* Pricing format
* Required supporting documents
* Contact person for questions

For example:

**Please submit your quotation as a PDF by August 10, 2026, at 5:00 PM Eastern Time. Use “Quotation for RFQ-2026-1048” as the email subject.**

### Request a complete cost breakdown

Do not ask only for a total price.

Ask vendors to separate:

* Unit price
* Total product or service cost
* Setup fees
* Tooling
* Samples
* Installation
* Shipping
* Taxes
* Duties
* Packaging
* Recurring charges
* Optional costs

This gives you a more accurate view of the total purchase cost.

## Step 3: Standardize the Vendor Responses

A good RFQ tells suppliers how to structure their quotations.

This reduces the amount of manual work required after the responses arrive.

Without a standard format, one supplier may provide pricing in an Excel file while another sends a paragraph inside an email. A third may send a PDF that combines shipping, taxes, and product prices into one total.

These responses are difficult to compare.

### Use a vendor pricing table

Ask every supplier to complete the same fields.

| Item | Description          |   Quantity |       Unit Price |      Total Price | Lead Time        | Warranty         |
| ---- | -------------------- | ---------: | ---------------: | ---------------: | ---------------- | ---------------- |
| 1    | [Product or service] | [Quantity] | Vendor completes | Vendor completes | Vendor completes | Vendor completes |

Add separate fields for:

* Shipping
* Taxes
* Discounts
* Payment terms
* Quote validity
* Minimum order quantity
* Additional fees
* Assumptions

### Ask vendors to identify exclusions

Suppliers should clearly state what is not included.

Common exclusions may include:

* Freight
* Installation
* Training
* Customs duties
* Packaging
* Maintenance
* Travel expenses
* After-sales support
* Replacement parts

A low quotation may become expensive once excluded costs are added.

### Request alternatives separately

Some vendors may recommend a different product, material, or service level.

Allow alternatives when they could create value, but ask the supplier to quote them separately from the main requirement.

This keeps the original offers comparable.

## Step 4: Compare Vendors Using Consistent Evaluation Criteria

Once the quotations arrive, compare every vendor using the same criteria.

Do not begin by looking only at price.

A professional vendor evaluation should consider cost, quality, delivery, risk, and service.

### Price and total cost

Review:

* Unit price
* Total price
* Shipping
* Taxes
* Duties
* Tooling
* Setup fees
* Recurring costs
* Payment terms

Calculate the total landed cost rather than comparing only the product price.

### Quality and compliance

Confirm whether the vendor meets:

* Product specifications
* Material requirements
* Quality standards
* Certification requirements
* Inspection requirements
* Warranty expectations

A lower price has little value if the product does not meet your requirements.

### Delivery and capacity

Compare:

* Product availability
* Production lead time
* Shipping time
* Final delivery date
* Order capacity
* Ability to support future volume
* Expedited options

A supplier may offer excellent pricing but be unable to meet your deadline.

### Experience and reliability

Review:

* Years in business
* Relevant industry experience
* Similar projects
* Customer references
* Online reputation
* Financial stability
* Communication quality
* Responsiveness during the RFQ process

The quotation process itself can reveal how the vendor may perform later.

A supplier that misses deadlines, ignores questions, or submits incomplete documents may create similar problems after receiving the order.

### Commercial terms

Compare:

* Deposit requirements
* Payment period
* Quote validity
* Cancellation terms
* Return policy
* Warranty
* Support
* Contract conditions

Commercial terms can significantly change the true value of an offer.

## Step 5: Use a Vendor Scorecard

A vendor scorecard turns a subjective decision into a structured comparison.

Assign each criterion a weight based on its importance.

For example:

| Evaluation Criterion         | Weight |
| ---------------------------- | -----: |
| Total cost                   |    30% |
| Quality and compliance       |    25% |
| Delivery and lead time       |    20% |
| Experience and reliability   |    15% |
| Payment and commercial terms |    10% |

Score each vendor from 1 to 5.

* 1: Poor
* 2: Below expectations
* 3: Acceptable
* 4: Strong
* 5: Excellent

Multiply the score by the assigned weight.

### Example vendor scorecard

| Criterion        | Weight | Vendor A | Vendor B | Vendor C |
| ---------------- | -----: | -------: | -------: | -------: |
| Total cost       |    30% |        5 |        4 |        3 |
| Quality          |    25% |        3 |        5 |        4 |
| Delivery         |    20% |        3 |        4 |        5 |
| Experience       |    15% |        4 |        5 |        3 |
| Commercial terms |    10% |        3 |        4 |        5 |

The scorecard does not make the decision automatically.

It helps your team understand why one vendor may be a better overall fit.

### Add a mandatory requirements check

Before calculating the final score, confirm whether each supplier meets all mandatory requirements.

Examples include:

* Required certification
* Delivery deadline
* Technical specification
* Minimum warranty
* Geographic coverage
* Required insurance

A vendor that fails a critical requirement may need to be removed from consideration regardless of its score.

## Step 6: Clarify, Select, and Document the Decision

The first quotation may not contain everything you need.

Create a structured clarification process before selecting a supplier.

### Send clarification questions

Ask about:

* Missing prices
* Unclear delivery dates
* Product substitutions
* Shipping exclusions
* Payment requirements
* Warranty details
* Quote validity
* Technical differences

Keep questions specific.

Avoid reopening the complete RFQ unless the original requirements have changed.

### Share important updates fairly

When one vendor asks a question that affects every supplier, consider sharing the clarification with all participants.

This supports a fair evaluation.

For example, if the delivery date changes, every vendor should have the opportunity to revise the quotation.

### Request a best and final offer

For higher-value purchases, you may ask shortlisted vendors to submit a final offer.

This gives suppliers a chance to improve:

* Pricing
* Payment terms
* Delivery
* Warranty
* Support
* Commercial conditions

Do not use this process only to force unrealistic discounts.

The goal is to confirm the supplier’s strongest complete offer.

### Document why the supplier was selected

Record:

* Vendors invited
* Quotations received
* Evaluation criteria
* Scores
* Clarifications
* Risks
* Final decision
* Reason for selection
* Required approvals

This creates a useful record for management and future purchases.

### Notify participating vendors

Send a professional award email to the selected supplier.

Thank unsuccessful vendors for their time and keep the communication respectful.

A vendor that was not suitable for this purchase may be valuable for a future requirement.

## Common Vendor Evaluation Mistakes

### Choosing the lowest quote automatically

The lowest price may exclude shipping, setup, support, or other important costs.

Compare the full commercial offer.

### Changing requirements during the process

When requirements change, suppliers may be quoting different scopes.

Issue a clear revision and give every vendor the same information.

### Comparing different products

One vendor may quote the exact requested item while another offers a lower-cost alternative.

Confirm technical compliance before comparing prices.

### Ignoring payment terms

A low price with full advance payment may be less attractive than a slightly higher price with Net 30 terms.

### Losing track of attachments

Vendor quotations may arrive with product sheets, certificates, terms, and revised files.

Keep every document connected to the correct supplier and RFQ.

### Depending on memory

Do not rely on email stars, personal notes, or one employee’s memory.

Use a scorecard and documented evaluation process.

No. The final decision should consider quality, delivery, risk, support, payment terms, and total cost.
